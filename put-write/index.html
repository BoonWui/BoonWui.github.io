Put-Write Desk

Once a month, sell a SPY put about a month out and well below today’s price. Buyers of these puts pay more than the crashes they insure against have cost on average, so the seller keeps the premium in most months. This page picks the strike from the live option chain, sizes it to your account, tracks what you’ve sold, and shows how the rule did since 1991 — including the months it lost.

Rules to trade it by

  • When. Once a month, on the first US trading day after the previous put expires (or the third Friday’s expiry), sell the put the ticket shows: the expiry closest to 30 days out, at the target delta. Use a limit order at the mid price; lower it a few cents if it doesn’t fill within 15 minutes.
  • Size. Cash-secured means keeping strike × 100 in cash (in T-bills or a money-market fund) for each put. Never sell more puts than the cash covers: the occasional big loss is survivable only without leverage. With spreads, put no more than 10% of the account at risk in any month — a spread can lose everything at risk in one crash, as the test shows if you raise “Account used”.
  • Hold to expiry. That is what was tested. If SPY is above the strike at expiry the put expires worthless and you keep the premium. If it is below, you either buy SPY at the strike (assignment) or close the put the day before expiry; the loss is the same.
  • Trend switch. When SPY is below its 200-day average, drop to the lower delta (further from the price) or skip the month. Most of the worst months came during falling markets.
  • Expect losing months. About 1 month in 10 loses, and once or twice a decade a month takes back a year or more of premium (October 2008, March 2020). The rule is still ahead over full cycles only if you keep selling after those months.
  • Small accounts. One SPY put needs about $60,000–$70,000 of cash. With less, use the put-spread setting: buy a second, lower put so the most you can lose is the gap between strikes.

How the test works and its limits

  • Every 21 trading days since 1991, sell a 30-day S&P 500 put at the target delta and hold it to expiry; the cash securing it earns the T-bill rate. A spread also buys the lower put.
  • Historical option prices are modelled, not real quotes: Black-Scholes with at-the-money volatility of 88% of the VIX and a skew of 0.6 volatility points per 1% out of the money, which matches typical S&P put prices. Each option then pays a spread cost of 3% of its price. Real fills can be worse in a panic.
  • The test cannot show a market crash bigger than any since 1991 in a single month. Size for a 25% drop in a month, not for the average.
  • Tax. SPY is a US security: Malaysian residents pay no US tax on option premiums, but brokers may withhold on assignment-related dividends. XSP (mini S&P index options, 1/10 the size of SPX, cash-settled) is an alternative with no assignment of shares.
  • Live quotes come from Yahoo Finance, refreshed three times each US trading day; they are 15–20 minutes delayed in the session. Check the price in your broker before sending the order.

Not financial advice. Options can lose more than the premium received.
